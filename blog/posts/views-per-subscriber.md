Subscriber count is the number creators celebrate and the number that tells you least. Views per subscriber is the one that tells you whether those subscribers are a community or a list.

## The calculation

> Views per subscriber = average views on recent uploads / subscribers x 100

Use the last ten uploads rather than a single video, and exclude anything that went unusually viral, because one runaway video will flatter the result for months.

A channel with 40,000 subscribers averaging 8,000 views per upload is at 20%. Our [subscriber count checker](../../creator-tools/youtube-subscriber-count-checker/) works this out automatically from the official API.

## What a healthy figure looks like

| Views per subscriber | Read as |
|---|---|
| 30% and above | Strong. Subscribers genuinely turn up for uploads |
| 10% to 30% | Normal for most active channels |
| Under 10% | Many subscribers are inactive, or your views come from elsewhere |

These bands are what our channel tools use, and they need context to be useful.

## Why the figure falls as a channel grows

Every channel's ratio declines over time, and that is not a failure. Someone who subscribed three years ago has a feed full of channels now. Some have stopped using YouTube. Some subscribed for one specific video and never wanted the rest.

A 5,000-subscriber channel often sits above 40%, because nearly everyone who subscribed did so recently and still cares. A two-million-subscriber channel sitting at 8% may be perfectly healthy, because 160,000 people per upload is a substantial audience by any measure.

Compare a channel with its own past, and with channels of similar size and format. Comparing across sizes tells you nothing.

## When a low figure is genuinely fine

**Search-driven channels.** Tutorials and how-to content collect views for years from people who arrive with a question and leave with an answer. They never subscribe, and they were never going to. A channel like this can run under 10% and be thriving.

**Shorts-heavy channels.** Shorts subscribers are converted in an autoplaying feed. Many never open a long video. If your Shorts share is high, expect a low ratio and read it alongside [how Shorts earnings work](../how-much-does-youtube-pay-for-shorts/).

**Very old channels.** Twelve years of accumulated subscribers includes a lot of people who have moved on.

## When a low figure is a warning

**A sudden drop.** If the ratio was 25% six months ago and is 9% now with no change in format, something broke. Usually it is upload consistency, a pivot the audience did not follow, or thumbnails that stopped earning clicks.

**Bought subscribers.** Purchased subscribers never watch anything, so the ratio collapses and the engagement rate collapses with it. If both are far below what the size predicts, that is the pattern.

**High subscriber growth with flat views.** New subscribers arriving while views stay still means you are converting people who do not stay. This is the most common invisible problem on channels that suddenly grew from one hit.

## Read it with engagement rate

The two numbers answer different questions and are strongest together:

- **Views per subscriber** asks whether people show up.
- **[Engagement rate](../youtube-engagement-rate-benchmarks/)** asks whether the people who showed up reacted.

Both healthy means a real audience. Both weak, at a size where that is unusual, is the fake-audience fingerprint. High views per subscriber with low engagement usually means search traffic. Low views per subscriber with high engagement means a small loyal core inside a large dormant list, which is a good position to grow from.

## What to do about a falling ratio

1. **Check consistency first.** Irregular uploads train the algorithm and the audience to stop expecting you.
2. **Look at your thumbnails against your own back catalogue.** Compare the click appeal of your recent set with the videos from when the ratio was healthy.
3. **Ask whether you drifted.** Audiences subscribe to a specific promise. Pivots cost the part of the audience that came for the old one, and that is sometimes the right trade, but make it deliberately.
4. **Stop counting subscribers as the goal.** Average views per upload is the number that pays, both in ad revenue and in what a brand will pay you. [Sponsorship pricing](../how-much-to-charge-for-a-sponsored-youtube-video/) is built on average views, not subscribers.
