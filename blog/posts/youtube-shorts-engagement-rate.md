A good engagement rate on YouTube Shorts is **4% to 6% of views**, and above 6% is excellent, on the same scale Passive Array uses for every YouTube video: (likes + comments) / views x 100. YouTube does not publish an official Shorts benchmark, so compare a Short with your own other Shorts first. And read every Shorts rate knowing that since 31 March 2025 YouTube counts a Shorts view on every start or replay, which makes the public rate lower than it would have been under the old count.

The [YouTube engagement rate calculator](../../creator-tools/youtube-engagement-rate-calculator/) works out the rate for any channel from public data, with no login. This guide explains the formula, how YouTube's view-counting changes affect it, what "good" means for Shorts, and the metrics in YouTube Studio that tell you more than the rate can.

## How to calculate a YouTube Shorts engagement rate

Passive Array measures YouTube engagement **by views**, with the same formula as our [YouTube engagement rate benchmarks](../youtube-engagement-rate-benchmarks/):

> Engagement rate = (likes + comments) / views x 100

Shares and dislikes are left out because YouTube does not make them public, so the formula works for any Short, yours or someone else's. Likes and comments count equally.

### For one Short

1. Open the Short and note its **views**, **likes** and **comments**.
2. Add likes and comments.
3. Divide by views and multiply by 100.

### For a channel's Shorts

1. Take the channel's 10 most recent Shorts. Skip anything older than a few months unless the channel posts rarely.
2. Add up views, likes and comments across all 10.
3. Divide total likes plus comments by total views, and multiply by 100.

Adding up totals first, rather than averaging ten separate rates, stops one tiny Short with 300 views from pulling the result around.

The calculator averages a channel's last 10 uploads of **any** kind. For a channel that posts only Shorts, that is its Shorts engagement rate. For a channel that mixes Shorts and long videos, the result blends the two, so work out the Shorts rate by hand with the steps above, or compare the calculator's result with the mix of uploads it used.

### A worked example

Say a Short has 120,000 views, 4,800 likes and 90 comments. This is an example:

- Likes plus comments: 4,800 + 90 = 4,890
- Engagement rate: 4,890 / 120,000 x 100 = **4.1%**, which grades **Good**

## What is a good engagement rate on YouTube Shorts?

The same scale applies to Shorts and long videos on every YouTube tool on this site and in the Passive Array for YouTube extension:

| Engagement by views | Grade |
|---|---|
| Under 1% | Low |
| 1% to 2% | Below average |
| 2% to 4% | Average |
| 4% to 6% | Good |
| Above 6% | Excellent |

This is our grading scale, not a YouTube figure. YouTube does not publish engagement benchmarks for Shorts or long-form, and figures from industry reports vary with how each one counts views. Treat any single "average Shorts engagement rate" you read with care, and check which formula and which view count it used.

### Shorts and long videos are not comparable

Shorts usually score higher than long videos on the same channel, because the like button is on screen for the whole Short and the viewer is already holding the phone. A channel at 2% on long videos and 5% on Shorts is not "better at Shorts" by that comparison alone. Compare Shorts with Shorts and long videos with long videos.

### Size changes the picture

Small channels usually post higher rates than large ones, on Shorts as on long-form. Most viewers of a small channel's Shorts are people who already like the creator. When a Short travels far through the Shorts feed, it reaches people who watch once and swipe on, and the rate falls even though the Short is a success. A 1.5% rate on a Short with 3 million views can be a better result than 8% on a Short with 2,000 views.

## How YouTube counts Shorts views, and why it matters

Your engagement rate has views at the bottom of the fraction, so the way YouTube counts a view changes the rate directly. It has changed twice.

| Date | What changed, per YouTube Help |
|---|---|
| 31 March 2025 | Shorts views began counting "the number of times a Short starts to play or replay, with no minimum watch time requirement." The previous metric was kept in YouTube Analytics as **Engaged views**. |
| 24 August 2026 | Views are counted "the moment a video starts to play across all formats, including Shorts", long-form videos and live streams. |

YouTube says these changes do not affect Partner Program earnings or eligibility. Shorts ad revenue sharing is based on engaged Shorts views. Our guide to [how YouTube counts views](../how-youtube-counts-views/) explains the full rules, including which views YouTube filters out.

### What this does to the rate

Because public views now include every start and replay, including people who swiped away almost at once, the number at the bottom of the fraction is bigger than it used to be for the same Short. The same likes and comments divided by a larger view count give a lower rate.

Three practical consequences:

- **Do not compare a Short from before April 2025 with one from after.** They were counted on different rules. Compare recent Shorts with recent Shorts.
- **A falling rate after March 2025 is not necessarily a falling audience.** Check whether likes and comments per Short also fell. If they held steady while views rose, the count changed, not the response.
- **Loops inflate views but not engaged views.** A short, loopable Short can collect many replays. Each replay can add a view, while YouTube Help says engaged views do not include loops.

## Engaged views: a second rate for your own Shorts

For your own channel, YouTube Studio shows **engaged views** for each Short. YouTube Help defines them as "how many times viewers stayed to watch past the initial seconds, not including any loops." YouTube's August 2026 explainer adds that "the vast majority of analytics remain anchored on engaged views", including click-through rate, average view duration and retention.

That gives you a second, private version of the rate:

> Engaged engagement rate = (likes + comments) / engaged views x 100

Using the worked example above, if that Short had 70,000 engaged views out of 120,000 views, the rate on engaged views would be 4,890 / 70,000 = **7.0%**, against 4.1% on public views.

Keep the two apart. The rate on engaged views is useful for comparing your own Shorts, because it ignores swipes and loops. It cannot be compared with anyone else's channel, because only the channel owner can see engaged views, and it should never be graded on the public scale above. Every public tool, including ours, has to use public views.

| Denominator | Who can see it | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| Views (public) | Anyone | Comparing channels, sponsorship checks, the grade on this site |
| Engaged views | Channel owner, in YouTube Studio | Comparing your own Shorts with each other |
| Subscribers | Anyone, if the count is public | How much of the subscribed audience reacts; rarely useful for Shorts |

## The Shorts metrics that matter more than the rate

Engagement rate tells you how viewers reacted. For Shorts, YouTube Studio has metrics that show something earlier: whether viewers stayed at all.

### Stayed to watch

YouTube Help defines **Stayed to watch** as "the percentage of times viewers stayed to watch past the initial seconds of a Short." It sits in the Reach tab for Shorts. Earlier versions of YouTube Studio called a similar metric "Viewed (vs. swiped away)", and many creators still use that name.

This is the first gate. A Short that most viewers swipe away cannot build a strong engagement rate, because few people stay long enough to tap like. Open **YouTube Studio > Analytics > Content > Shorts** and compare Stayed to watch across your recent Shorts before you look at likes.

### Engaged views and average view duration

YouTube Help says average view duration for Shorts is "calculated from engaged views and their corresponding watch time", so it measures the viewers who stayed, not everyone who saw the first frame. Together with Stayed to watch, it shows whether a Short holds people once they decide to watch.

### Reading them together

| What you see | What it usually means | What to try |
|---|---|---|
| Low Stayed to watch, low engagement | The opening loses people | Start with the payoff or the most interesting frame |
| High Stayed to watch, low engagement | People watch but have no reason to react | A specific question for comments, a clearer ending |
| High engagement, few views | The Short works for the people who see it | More Shorts in the same format and topic |
| Many views, falling rate | The Short reached a wide audience beyond your usual viewers | Normal for a breakout; check subscribers gained from it |
| High views from loops, few engaged views | Replays inflate the count | Judge it on engaged views, not public views |

## How to improve your Shorts engagement rate

These steps work on the parts of the formula you control: whether people stay, and whether they react.

### 1. Earn the first seconds

Nobody likes a Short they swiped away from. Show the result, the problem or the surprising moment first. Cut greetings, logos and "wait for it" set-ups. Then check Stayed to watch on your next five Shorts against your last five.

### 2. Ask one specific question

"Comment below" is easy to ignore. "Which of these two would you pick, left or right?" is easy to answer. A specific question is the most direct way to raise the comment half of the formula, and replies to comments bring people back to the Short.

### 3. Pin a comment that continues the Short

A pinned comment with the answer, the next step or the question you want people to argue about gives viewers a reason to open the comments and add their own.

### 4. End on a reason to like or subscribe

The like button is on screen the whole time. Give people a reason to press it at the moment the Short pays off, not at the start, when they have not decided yet.

### 5. Make Shorts for one audience

Shorts are recommended to people likely to enjoy them. A channel whose Shorts jump between unrelated topics is tested on a different audience each time, and none of them becomes a returning viewer. Returning viewers are the ones who like and comment. Our guide to [growing a channel with Shorts](../how-to-grow-youtube-channel-with-shorts/) covers how to connect Shorts to long-form videos, and [YouTube Shorts SEO](../youtube-shorts-seo/) covers how Shorts get found in search.

### 6. Do not chase the rate with tricks

YouTube's fake engagement policy says it "doesn't allow anything that artificially increases the number of views, likes, comments, or other metrics", and bought likes come from people who will never watch your Shorts again. Asking viewers to like or comment is allowed; paying for it or trading it is not. YouTube Help also notes that some likes may be marked invalid and periodically removed from the counts, so artificial likes can disappear later anyway.

## Mistakes when judging Shorts engagement

- **Comparing Shorts with long videos.** Different formats, different rates. Grade each against its own kind.
- **Comparing with TikTok rates.** Our TikTok formula counts shares as well as likes and comments, and TikTok's audience behaves differently. A Shorts rate and a [TikTok engagement rate](../tiktok-engagement-rate-benchmarks/) are not on the same scale.
- **Mixing old and new view counts.** Shorts posted before the March 2025 change were counted on a different rule.
- **Grading a rate on engaged views with the public scale.** It will look better than it is next to other channels.
- **Reading one Short.** A single Short can swing far above or below the channel's usual rate. Use the last 10.
- **Ignoring comments being off.** If comments are disabled, half the formula disappears and the rate drops.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a good YouTube Shorts engagement rate?

On Passive Array's scale, 4% to 6% of views is good and above 6% is excellent, measured as (likes + comments) / views x 100. YouTube does not publish an official benchmark, so compare a Short with your own recent Shorts and with channels of a similar size.

### What is the average engagement rate for YouTube Shorts?

There is no official average. Published figures vary because they use different formulas and, since March 2025, a different way of counting Shorts views. On our scale, 2% to 4% of views is average, but small channels often score higher and very large Shorts lower.

### How do you calculate engagement rate on YouTube Shorts?

Add the Short's likes and comments, divide by its views and multiply by 100. For a channel, add up likes, comments and views across its last 10 Shorts first, then apply the same formula. A free YouTube engagement rate calculator does this for any channel.

### Why did my Shorts engagement rate drop?

Often because of how views are counted, not because viewers reacted less. Since 31 March 2025 YouTube counts a Shorts view on every start or replay with no minimum watch time, which raises views and lowers the rate. Check whether likes and comments per Short also fell before you change anything.

### Should I use views or engaged views for Shorts engagement rate?

Use public views when comparing with other channels, because that is all anyone else can see. Use engaged views, from YouTube Studio, to compare your own Shorts with each other, since it ignores quick swipes and loops. Never mix the two in one comparison.

### Are Shorts engagement rates higher than long videos?

Usually, yes. The like button is on screen for the whole Short, so a larger share of viewers tap it. That is why Shorts and long videos should be graded separately, even on the same channel.

### Is there a benchmark for YouTube Shorts engagement by channel size?

YouTube does not publish one. As a rule of thumb, smaller channels post higher rates because most viewers are already fans, and rates fall when a Short reaches a wide audience through the Shorts feed. Compare with channels of a similar size and format.
